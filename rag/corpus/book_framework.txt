THE INTERFACE IS NOT THE SYSTEM — Six Failure Patterns
Source: Nelly Pourmehr, Chapters 1-2.

FAILURE PATTERN 1: CONFUSING A FEATURE WITH A STRATEGY
"We need AI in our product" sounds like strategy but is a feature
request dressed up in urgency. Adding AI without a clear problem to
solve means nobody has answered what user problem becomes solvable
that wasn't before, or what workflow transforms in a way that matters.
Teams skip this question because answering it honestly might mean
admitting they don't need AI at all.

FAILURE PATTERN 2: OPTIMIZING FOR ACCURACY INSTEAD OF USEFULNESS
Accuracy is measurable and seductive, it goes in a slide deck. But
accuracy measures the model, not the product. A 95% accurate system
that destroys user trust on the first error is worse than an 80%
accurate system that helps users catch and correct mistakes easily.
The real question: when the AI is wrong, can the user tell? Can they
recover without starting over? Users experience consequences, not
accuracy percentages.

FAILURE PATTERN 3: TREATING UNCERTAINTY LIKE A BUG
Traditional software is deterministic; AI breaks that contract. Design
teams try to hide the uncertainty, stripping confidence scores because
"users don't understand them," or auto-applying AI suggestions to
streamline the flow. This creates interfaces that look deterministic
even though the system underneath is not. The failure isn't the
uncertainty itself, it's pretending the uncertainty doesn't exist.

FAILURE PATTERN 4: SKIPPING THE WORKFLOW IMPACT ANALYSIS
An AI feature doesn't just slot into an existing workflow, it
transforms it, or it should. Questions that go unasked: does it
eliminate steps or add new ones? Does it shift when decisions get
made? Does it change who's involved? Does it create new dependencies?
If an AI feature slides seamlessly into the existing workflow with no
friction, it's probably not doing anything important enough to matter.

FAILURE PATTERN 5: BRINGING IN UX AFTER THE DECISIONS ARE MADE
By the time UX is asked to "make it easy to use," the core decisions,
what the AI predicts, what inputs it needs, what outputs it gives, are
already locked in. These are UX decisions made in engineering meetings
in disguise. Starting with UX doesn't slow teams down, it prevents
them from building the wrong thing quickly.

FAILURE PATTERN 6: ASSUMING USERS WANT WHAT YOU'RE BUILDING
Technically impressive systems can solve a problem users don't
actually have. This happens when nobody asked real end users, or the
answer got reinterpreted, or the wrong people were asked (managers
instead of the people doing the work daily). The failure isn't
technical, it's empathy: the team fell in love with their solution
before understanding the problem.

WHEN AI SHOULD NOT BE USED AT ALL (disqualifying conditions)
- The current workflow is already fast and reliable; users complete
  the task in seconds with full confidence.
- Users need to understand exactly how a decision was made: legal
  research, financial compliance, medical diagnosis, audit trails.
  AI that can't explain its reasoning in verifiable terms makes these
  jobs impossible, not easier.
- Failure breaks critical trust: being wrong once costs more than many
  correct outputs earn back. The math doesn't favor probabilistic
  systems when errors are unforgivable.
- It forces users to change their workflow to feed the model. The AI
  must deliver extraordinary value to justify that tax. Most don't.

RED FLAGS IN AI PRODUCT IDEAS
- "We'll figure out UX later" / "Users will learn to trust it over
  time" — trust is earned by being trustworthy, not owed with exposure.
- "The AI is good enough that users won't need to verify" — when users
  can't easily verify output, they either trust blindly, verify
  thoroughly (killing time savings), or don't use it at all.
- "We can educate users about the limitations" — education is not a
  substitute for good design.
- "It works in the demo" — demos use ideal conditions; real usage
  happens under time pressure with messy data and skeptical users.
- "We're using AI because everyone else is" — checks a marketing box
  while creating a support burden.

RISK ASSESSMENT: THE FOUR DIMENSIONS
1. Impact — what happens when the AI is wrong? Low-impact errors are
   inconvenient; high-impact errors are costly, embarrassing, harmful.
2. Reversibility — how easily can users undo or correct AI decisions?
3. User confidence — can users tell when they should trust the AI, or
   does it appear equally confident whether right or wrong?
4. Downstream effects — do results stay contained, or cascade, affect
   other people, trigger irreversible processes, create obligations?

Low-risk AI assists decisions (suggesting, recommending, categorizing
with visible, movable results). High-risk AI makes decisions (auto-
sending, rejecting candidates without review, adjusting dosages,
purchasing above a threshold) where users lose control and correction
is difficult or impossible.

THE 9-QUESTION VALIDATION SCORECARD
1. Does this problem genuinely require AI rather than deterministic
   logic or rules?
2. Is the decision or task clearly defined and repeatable at scale?
3. Is there a clear, measurable definition of success for this outcome?
4. Is the impact of error acceptable for users and the business?
5. Is the decision reversible if the system is wrong?
6. Can users understand, question, or contextualize the AI's output?
7. Is reliable, representative data available and sustainable over time?
8. Can humans intervene, override, or correct the system when needed?
9. Is the organization prepared to maintain, monitor, and recalibrate
   this system long-term?
Three or more "Fail" answers means the use case should be reconsidered
or redesigned before introducing AI.

CORE PRINCIPLE
The hard part of AI products isn't getting the AI to work. It's
knowing what working actually means. Better prompts cannot fix a bad
use case, and a traceable execution path does not guarantee the
correctness of the judgment calls that drive it.
