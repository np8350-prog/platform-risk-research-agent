EU AI ACT AND NIST AI RMF — AI-SPECIFIC COMPLIANCE REFERENCE

This file covers two AI-specific frameworks, separate from general data and
security compliance (SOC2, GDPR, ISO 27001, HIPAA, PCI, FedRAMP). These
frameworks govern AI systems as AI systems: what they decide, who they
affect, and how that risk is classified and managed. Use this material when
scoring Compliance Posture for a vendor whose product involves automated
decisions, generative output, or algorithmic scoring of people.

---

EU AI ACT — RISK TIERS

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, in force since February 2025, phasing in through
2027. Every AI system placed on the EU market falls into one of four risk
tiers. The tier determines the compliance burden, not the vendor's own
description of their product.

Unacceptable risk: banned outright, no compliance pathway exists. Covers
practices like subliminal manipulation, exploiting vulnerabilities of
specific groups (age, disability, economic situation), social scoring
systems, and most real-time biometric identification in public spaces.
A vendor operating in this category is disqualifying, not just high risk.

High risk: permitted, but under strict conditions. Applies to AI used in
critical infrastructure, education, employment decisions (hiring, firing,
performance scoring), access to essential services (credit scoring,
insurance, benefits), law enforcement, migration, and administration of
justice. High-risk systems require a conformity assessment, technical
documentation, EU database registration, and human oversight mechanisms
before they can be sold or deployed.

Limited risk: permitted, with a transparency requirement. Covers systems
that interact directly with users, like chatbots or generative content
tools. The only obligation is disclosure: users must be told they're
interacting with AI, or that content is AI-generated (this includes
deepfake labeling).

Minimal risk: permitted, no mandatory obligations. Covers most everyday AI
applications, such as spam filters or AI features in productivity tools
that don't touch the categories above.

Classification test for scoring a vendor:
1. Does it match a prohibited practice under Article 5? If yes, unacceptable
   risk, disqualifying.
2. Does it match an Annex III use case (the high-risk list above)? If yes,
   high risk, requires proof of conformity assessment and oversight
   mechanisms.
3. Does it interact with users without disclosing it's AI? If yes, limited
   risk, requires a transparency check only.
4. None of the above: minimal risk, no specific EU AI Act obligation.

Penalties for non-compliance scale up to EUR 35 million or 7% of global
annual turnover, whichever is higher. This is meaningfully steeper than
GDPR penalties, worth flagging when a vendor's use case lands in the
high-risk tier.

NIST AI RMF — FOUR FUNCTIONS

Published January 2023 by the US National Institute of Standards and
Technology. Voluntary, not certifiable, referenced by nist.gov/itl/
ai-risk-management-framework. Unlike SOC2 or ISO 27001, there's no audit
or certificate to check for. What to look for instead is whether a vendor
can describe their practices in terms of these four functions, which
signals real process rather than a marketing claim.

Govern: cross-cutting function that runs through the other three. Covers
whether the organization has a risk-aware culture, clear ownership of AI
risk decisions, and policies that assign accountability. A vendor with no
answer to "who owns AI risk internally" fails this function regardless of
what else they claim.

Map: understanding the AI system in its actual context, not just its
technical function. Covers identifying who's affected by the system,
what the system is meant to do, and where the potential harms or
blind spots are. This is where a vendor should be able to name specific
use cases their product is not appropriate for.

Measure: assessing and benchmarking identified risks with actual metrics,
not just intentions. Covers testing for reliability, bias, safety, and
security, and tracking these over time rather than as a one-time check
before launch.

Manage: allocating resources to treat the risks that Map and Measure
identified, and responding when something goes wrong. Covers incident
response, ongoing monitoring, and whether the vendor updates their
practices when new risks surface post-launch.

Compliance signal to look for: a vendor mentioning NIST AI RMF alignment
without being able to speak to any of the four functions specifically is a
weaker signal than a vendor with no NIST mention who can clearly describe
their governance, context-mapping, measurement, and incident response
practices in their own words. The framework is the vocabulary, not the
proof.

Note: some US states now reference NIST AI RMF directly. Colorado's AI Act
treats NIST AI RMF alignment (or ISO/IEC 42001 certification) as an
affirmative defense in liability cases. This raises the practical stakes
of NIST alignment beyond "nice to have" for vendors selling into regulated
US markets.

HOW THIS MAPS TO COMPLIANCE POSTURE SCORING

Use EU AI Act tier classification when the vendor's product makes decisions
about people (hiring, credit, benefits, safety) or generates content shown
to end users. A vendor that can't say which tier they fall into is itself
a signal, not just missing data.

Use NIST AI RMF function coverage as a secondary signal, especially for
US-based or US-market vendors. Treat a vendor's own claim of "NIST aligned"
as self-reported evidence, same confidence tagging rule as any other
uncorroborated compliance claim. Independent confirmation (audit,
certification, documented incident response) is what moves it from
"limited evidence" to "strong evidence."
