CONTROLGAP — Governance and Risk Evaluation Framework
Source: Nelly Pourmehr, ControlGap pre-mortem tool.

Core question: what actually fails when an AI system fails? Not the
model. Not the interface. The system around it, and specifically,
where control, trust, and responsibility break down at scale.

FOUR FAILURE MODES TO EVALUATE IN ANY AI SYSTEM OR VENDOR

1. SILENT FAILURE
The system is wrong and the user does not know. No alert fires, no
flag appears, the output looks exactly like a correct output. This is
the most dangerous failure type in any vendor evaluation: does the
platform have any mechanism for surfacing when its own output should
not be trusted, or does a wrong answer look identical to a right one?

2. OVERTRUST FROM REPEATED ACCURACY
Users who have seen a system be correct multiple times stop checking
it. They lose their defensive posture exactly when an edge case
arrives. For a vendor evaluation: does the platform's design encourage
or discourage this drift, and does the vendor acknowledge it anywhere
in their documentation?

3. MISINTERPRETATION OF OUTPUT
A system outputs something technically correct that the user reads
incorrectly, a probability read as certainty, a suggestion followed as
a directive, a confidence score ignored or buried in the UI. This
matters most in vendor evaluation when the platform is embedded inside
someone else's workflow, since the misinterpretation risk transfers to
the buyer's own users.

4. FEEDBACK LOOP COLLAPSE
A system that learns from user behavior can drift silently if that
behavior is systematically biased, users accept more than they reject,
they don't report failures, positive outcomes get read as confirmation
even when unrelated. Relevant to any vendor whose platform improves
itself on customer usage data.

RISK LAYER STRUCTURE (applied to vendor/platform evaluation)

Hidden risks: risks that only become visible when reasoning about the
interaction between the vendor's system behavior and the buyer's user
behavior at scale, not risks a standard vendor security review would
surface on its own.

Long-term risks: risks that compound over time rather than trigger
once. Every long-term risk needs a named compounding factor, the
specific mechanism that makes it worse with continued use, not just a
static probability.

Responsibility gaps: the precise moment accountability transfers from
the vendor's system to the buyer's own team, and whether that transfer
is visible to the buyer when it happens. Vendors rarely state this
explicitly; it has to be inferred from the product's design and terms
of service.

Second-order effects: what accumulates after repeated use over time,
not what happens in a single incident. For a buyer evaluating a
vendor, this means asking what happens to their own business,
reputation, or compliance posture after months of dependency on this
platform, not just what happens if a single API call fails.

CORE EVALUATION PRINCIPLE
A vendor can be individually well-built and still be dangerous to
adopt if the responsibility gap between "vendor's system did something"
and "buyer's team is accountable for the outcome" is invisible to the
buyer at the moment they integrate it. This is the central question
Platform Risk evaluation exists to answer: not "is this vendor good,"
but "does the buyer know, at every point, where control actually sits."


COMPLIANCE AND REGULATORY REFERENCE (for Compliance Posture scoring)

SOC 2 (Type I vs Type II)
SOC 2 Type I assesses whether a vendor's controls are suitably
designed at a single point in time. SOC 2 Type II assesses whether
those controls actually operated effectively over a period, typically
6-12 months. Type II is the stronger signal, since it requires
sustained operation, not just a design review. A vendor citing "SOC 2
compliant" without specifying Type I or II, or without a visible
report date, is a weaker claim than one with a dated, specific
attestation. Ask: is the report available, current, and Type II?

GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation, EU)
Relevant vendor questions: Does the vendor act as a data processor or
data controller for the buyer's data? Is there a Data Processing
Agreement (DPA) available? Where is data stored and processed
(data residency matters for EU buyers)? Does the vendor support data
subject rights (access, deletion, portability) for data the buyer's
own users provide through the platform? A vendor that cannot answer
where data is stored, or has no DPA, is a compliance gap regardless of
marketing claims of "GDPR compliant."

ISO 27001
An information security management system standard. Certification
means a vendor has documented and audited security processes, not
that the vendor is immune to breaches. Check certificate validity
dates (certification lapses) and scope (a certification can cover
one product line and not another).

DATA BREACH AND INCIDENT DISCLOSURE
A vendor's public incident history matters less as an absolute count
and more as a pattern: was the vendor transparent when incidents
happened, how fast was disclosure, and what changed afterward? A
vendor with one disclosed, well-handled incident can be a better bet
than a vendor with no disclosed incidents and no visible security
practice, since the absence of disclosed incidents is not the same as
the absence of incidents.

VENDOR-AS-PROCESSOR RISK (specific to AI/no-code platforms)
When a buyer's data flows through a third-party AI platform, the
buyer's own compliance obligations do not transfer away, they extend
to cover the vendor's handling of that data. This is the responsibility
gap in Platform Risk terms: the buyer is accountable for outcomes
even when the vendor's system produced them. A platform that trains
on customer data by default, without an opt-out, is a materially
different risk than one that processes data only per-request with no
retention.

INTEGRATION RISK (specific to fintech buyers)
For a fintech buyer, ask: what happens to the buyer's own workflow if
this vendor's platform goes down, changes its API, or gets acquired?
A vendor deeply wired into a payment or compliance workflow with no
documented fallback path is a structural risk independent of how good
the vendor's own security posture is.
